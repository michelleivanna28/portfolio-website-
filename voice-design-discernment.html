Last month, I joined a hackathon with a couple of engineers.
What came out of it was a prototype, and somewhere between the first build and the version we actually submitted, I ended up turning an engineer's first product into a designer's first one.
The screenshots from that process taught me more than the hackathon itself did.
Because once I started looking closely at what the AI had generated on its own, I noticed the same handful of habits showing up again and again. So here they are, the tells I've picked up from working across Gamma, PPT generators, Firefly, GPT, Gemini, and now Claude Design, using our hackathon prototype as examples.
1.
Emoji everywhere
⤢
Icons and symbols take time to make well. Emojis don't. They give a screen some visual texture without anyone having to draw anything, which honestly makes sense as a shortcut. It's also one of the fastest ways to spot AI made work.
2.
Everything gets a card
⤢
Bento style, boxes around boxes, every piece of content wrapped in its own little container whether it needed one or not.
3.
The copy has a formula
‹
›
‹
›
This one took me longest to name but it's the most consistent. Once you actually read the words instead of scanning the layout, you start noticing the same pattern. Small caps eyebrow text sitting above almost everything. A header sized topic underneath it. Then a description that is mostly redundant.
4.
Category bubbles
⤢
Little pill shaped tags labeling things that didn't necessarily need labeling.
5.
The layout quietly breaks somewhere
⤢
Text that's too small in one spot, an element floating a little off, spacing that's almost right but not quite. Small things, but you feel them even when you can't name them.
6.
Last but not least, it looks perfect until you use it
‹
›
‹
›
This one actually mattered for our project. Hierarchy is something I think about instinctively now, what should someone notice first, what should they feel the second they land on a screen. Hackathon timelines don't leave room to nitpick, so this ended up being the biggest change I made, going back and fixing what people would actually feel when they opened it.
Field notes:
I can understand why all of this happens, though.
If not specified, the model is pulling from the most common examples of ‘good design’ it has seen, which makes it genuinely fast for getting a proof of concept out the door. What it doesn't have yet is a reason to make something feel like it belongs to one specific product instead of every product.
I also haven't come across an AI that writes exactly what I meant on the first try, it usually takes a few rounds of pushing back before the wording sounds like something a person would actually say.
That's the part a designer still does. AI can attempt it, and quickly. But someone still has to decide what this specific screen should feel like to this specific person, and right now that decision is still ours to make.
